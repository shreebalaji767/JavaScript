MATH WITHOUT THE MYSTERY

Maths classroom.

Learn maths from zero to advanced topics: number sense, algebra, functions, vectors, matrices, probability, statistics, calculus, complex numbers and numerical methods. Then connect useful ideas to JavaScript. No assumed knowledge; no shame for asking why.

🧠 Your learning path: start at the beginning

Stage 1 — Build number sense: place value, addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, factors and fractions. Stage 2 — Work with quantities: decimals, rounding, percentages, ratios and unit rates. Stage 3 — Think symbolically: expressions, equations, functions and logic. Stage 4 — Apply maths: geometry, statistics, probability and word problems. Stage 5 — Advanced maths: logarithms, sequences, systems, vectors, matrices, calculus, trigonometry, complex numbers, graph theory and numerical methods. Each lesson teaches the idea, shows worked examples, points out traps, and checks your understanding.

🧠 What maths do you actually need for JavaScript?

Start here: arithmetic, percentages, ratios, basic algebra, functions, and logic. Learn when relevant: binary/bitwise operations, statistics, geometry, and trigonometry. Specialist paths: calculus and advanced linear algebra are not required for most web development. Pick the topics that match what you want to build.

IDEAS HAVE A HISTORY

History classroom.

Understand not only how maths and JavaScript work, but why people invented them. Follow the problems, people, discoveries, and engineering decisions that shaped the tools you are learning today.

🧭
Learn the story, not just the dates.

Every era begins with a human problem. Follow the problem → idea → method → modern use. Dates are approximate where a discovery developed over centuries, and credit often belongs to many people and cultures—not one lone genius.

TIMELINE A

How humanity learned to count, measure, and reason

Maths did not appear as one finished textbook. It grew from trade, calendars, building, astronomy, philosophical proof, number systems, algebra, and the need to describe change.

c. 3000–1800 BCE

Counting becomes a tool of civilisation

Mesopotamian scribes developed sophisticated arithmetic and a base-60 place-value tradition; Egyptian scribes used practical arithmetic and geometry for administration, trade, and land measurement. These traditions were not identical, and both changed over time.

Why it matters nowPlace value and number systems let us represent large quantities and calculate systematically. Base 60 still echoes in 60 seconds per minute and 360 degrees in a circle.
Try itWrite 1 hour 25 minutes as minutes. Explain why 1 hour is 60 minutes, not 100.
c. 1800 BCE onward

Tables, algorithms, and repeatable procedures

Ancient Babylonian tablets recorded multiplication tables, reciprocal tables, and methods for solving practical numerical problems. A written procedure could be followed by another person—a powerful ancestor of an algorithm.

Why it matters nowAn algorithm is a finite, ordered method for solving a problem. JavaScript functions and loops are modern ways to express such procedures.
Try itWrite step-by-step instructions for finding the largest of three numbers. Test the steps on three different sets.
c. 600–300 BCE

Greek mathematics develops proof

Greek mathematicians including Thales, Pythagoras' school, and Euclid helped develop traditions of deductive reasoning. Euclid's Elements, compiled around 300 BCE, organised geometry through definitions, axioms, and proofs.

Why it matters nowA result is stronger when you can explain why it must follow, not merely observe that it worked once. Proof-like reasoning helps us test code and mathematical claims.
Try itDraw three right triangles of different sizes and test the Pythagorean relationship. Then explain why examples alone are not a general proof.
Ancient India · first millennium BCE and later

Number notation and calculation traditions grow

Indian mathematical traditions contributed to the development and transmission of the decimal place-value system and the use of zero as a number. The history unfolded over centuries; it was not a single invention on one date.

Why it matters nowIn a place-value system, the position of a digit changes its value. Zero can act as a placeholder and, in arithmetic, as a number with its own rules.
Try itCompare 205, 250, and 25. Explain what each zero does, and why 205 is not the same as 25.
c. 499 CE

Aryabhata connects calculation and astronomy

Indian mathematician and astronomer Aryabhata wrote the Aryabhatiya, covering topics such as arithmetic, algebraic methods, and trigonometry, with applications to astronomy.

Why it matters nowMathematical models turn observations into predictions. Modern simulations and software do the same, while depending on assumptions and measurement quality.
Try itMake a small table of an object's distance after 1, 2, 3, and 4 seconds at a constant speed. Describe the pattern.
628 CE

Brahmagupta writes rules for zero and negative numbers

In the Brahmasphutasiddhanta, Brahmagupta gave influential rules for arithmetic involving zero, debts and fortunes (a way to think about negative and positive quantities), and equations.

Why it matters nowNegative numbers model values below a reference point, debts, temperature changes, and movement in opposite directions. Zero requires special care: division by zero is not defined in ordinary arithmetic.
Try itModel a bank balance of −₹200 followed by a deposit of ₹350. Show the arithmetic and explain the final balance.
8th–13th centuries

Scholars translate, critique, and extend mathematics

Mathematical knowledge moved across languages and regions through translation, teaching, trade, and scholarship. Scholars working in the Islamic world preserved and extended Greek, Indian, and other traditions; al-Khwarizmi's 9th-century work helped shape algebra, and the word “algorithm” derives from the Latinised form of his name.

Why it matters nowKnowledge advances through communities that share, question, and improve methods. “Algebra” and “algorithm” preserve traces of this connected history.
Try itSolve x + 7 = 19 by undoing the addition. Write the same solution as a repeatable algorithm.
1202

Fibonacci helps spread Hindu-Arabic numerals in Europe

Leonardo of Pisa, known as Fibonacci, published Liber Abaci, introducing European merchants to calculation methods using Hindu-Arabic numerals. These numerals were not invented by Fibonacci; they reached Europe through a much longer exchange of knowledge.

Why it matters nowNotation affects how easy it is to calculate. Positional numerals make written arithmetic and algorithms far more practical than many older numeral systems.
Try itCalculate 47 × 16 using written multiplication, then use a decomposition such as 47 × (10 + 6). Compare the methods.
17th century

Algebra, analytic geometry, and calculus change the questions

Descartes and Fermat helped connect algebra with geometry. Newton and Leibniz independently developed foundational forms of calculus, providing tools for rates of change and accumulation. Their work built on earlier mathematical ideas.

Why it matters nowFunctions connect inputs to outputs; derivatives describe instantaneous rates of change, and integrals describe accumulation. These ideas underpin physics, optimisation, graphics, and scientific computing.
Try itRecord a moving object's distance every second. Estimate its speed over each interval and explain how shorter intervals improve the estimate.
17th–20th centuries

Probability, statistics, logic, and computation mature

Probability and statistics developed through many contributors and applications, from games of chance to astronomy, medicine, and public life. Formal logic and mathematical models of computation later helped clarify what procedures can be computed and how.

Why it matters nowStatistics helps us summarise uncertain data; logic gives us conditions; and computation turns formal procedures into executable instructions.
Try itFlip a coin 20 times, record heads and tails, and compare the observed fraction of heads with 1/2. Explain why a small sample need not match exactly.
Today

Mathematics powers software—and software helps explore maths

Computers can calculate, visualise, simulate, and search, but they still follow representations and algorithms designed by people. Floating-point arithmetic also means many decimal fractions are only approximations in binary.

Why it matters nowLearning maths history makes concepts less arbitrary: place value, algebra, functions, logic, and approximation each answer a real family of problems.
Try itRun console.log(0.1 + 0.2) in the practice lab. Explain why a tiny display difference is a representation issue, not evidence that addition has stopped working.
MINI PROJECT

Choose five timeline events. For each one, write the problem, the new idea or method, one worked example, and one modern use. Add a JavaScript object for each event and render the museum as a list sorted by date. Include a note about uncertainty or shared contributions wherever appropriate.

TIMELINE B

How JavaScript became the language of the web

JavaScript began as a way to make web pages interactive. Standardisation, better engines, server runtimes, modules, and asynchronous features helped it grow into a language used across browsers, servers, tools, and applications.

1989–1991

The Web is born as a system of linked documents

Tim Berners-Lee developed the foundations of the World Wide Web at CERN: HTML, URLs, HTTP, a browser, and a server. Early web pages were primarily documents, so developers soon wanted richer interaction.

Why it matters nowHTML describes document structure, CSS controls presentation, and JavaScript adds behaviour. They solve different but cooperating problems.
Try itOn any page you build, label one piece of structure, one visual style, and one behaviour. Explain which web technology should own each job.
1995

JavaScript is created for the browser

Brendan Eich created the first version at Netscape in 1995. It was developed very quickly for browser scripting and initially used the name Mocha, then LiveScript, before being renamed JavaScript. The JavaScript name was a marketing decision during Java's popularity; JavaScript and Java are different languages.

Why it matters nowThe language's original purpose explains its close relationship with browser events, documents, and user interaction—and why it can feel flexible in ways that surprise beginners.
Try itMake a button that changes a heading. Identify the event, the instruction that runs, and the visible result.
1997

ECMAScript gives the language a shared standard

ECMA International published the first ECMAScript specification in 1997. JavaScript is the best-known implementation of the ECMAScript language standard, alongside host-specific APIs provided by browsers or other runtimes.

Why it matters nowA standard helps engines agree on language behaviour. The language itself is distinct from browser APIs such as the DOM and from runtime APIs such as Node.js file access.
Try itSort features into “core JavaScript” (for example, arrays and functions) and “browser API” (for example, document.querySelector).
2000s

Dynamic pages become everyday web experiences

DOM scripting, events, and techniques often grouped under the name AJAX allowed pages to update parts of their content without a full reload. Libraries helped developers cope with browser differences.

Why it matters nowModern interactive apps still listen for events, update the DOM, and request data in the background—even though today's APIs are more capable.
Try itBuild a live character counter. Update a message on every input event without refreshing the page.
2009

Node.js brings JavaScript beyond the browser

Node.js made it practical to run JavaScript outside a browser, including on servers and in developer tools. It helped JavaScript become a full-stack language, though browser and Node.js APIs remain different.

Why it matters nowThe same language can run in different environments, but available APIs depend on where the code executes.
Try itCompare console.log("hello") in a browser console with the same statement in Node.js. Then list which environment provides document.
2015 · ECMAScript 2015 (ES6)

A major modernisation makes everyday code clearer

ES2015 introduced or standardised major features including let and const, arrow functions, classes, template literals, destructuring, modules, promises, and more. The language continued evolving through yearly editions.

Why it matters nowThese are core tools in modern tutorials and codebases. Learning the older ideas behind them helps you understand why the newer syntax exists.
Try itWrite a function declaration and an arrow function for the same calculation. Compare their syntax, then learn when their behaviour differs.
2017

async/await makes asynchronous code easier to read

Async functions and await became part of ECMAScript 2017, building on Promises. They let developers express many asynchronous workflows in a style that reads more like sequential code.

Why it matters nowFetching data, waiting for a response, and handling failures are central to modern apps. await does not freeze the whole browser while the operation completes.
Try itCompare a Promise chain with an async function that awaits the same result. Add a try/catch error path.
2010s–2020s

Modules, tooling, and frameworks reshape development

ES modules, package managers, bundlers, testing tools, browser APIs, and UI frameworks changed how large applications are organised. TypeScript also became widely used as a typed language that builds on JavaScript, but it is not itself the ECMAScript standard.

Why it matters nowGood structure helps projects grow without becoming a spaghetti monster. You can still learn the foundations with plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript.
Try itSplit a tiny calculator into one module for pure calculations and another for page interaction. Explain why the separation helps testing.
Today

JavaScript is a living language and ecosystem

ECMAScript continues to evolve through a public standards process. Browser engines and server runtimes implement features over time, so compatibility and clear documentation remain important.

Why it matters nowDo not learn JavaScript as a pile of magic words. Learn the core model, practise with projects, and use reliable documentation to check details.
Try itPick one feature you learned, explain the problem it solves, write a small demo, and check its current support in MDN documentation.
MINI PROJECT

How to study history so it actually sticks

1. Start with the problem

Ask what people needed to count, calculate, prove, automate, or make interactive.

2. Follow the idea

Explain what changed and how the new method improved or extended earlier work.

3. Rebuild it yourself

Write a small calculation or program that demonstrates the idea without copying the explanation.

4. Connect past to present

Name the modern concept, tool, or code pattern that still carries the idea forward.
